CHAPTER ONE
History & Spiritual Purpose
The Harp of King David in Ethiopia
Chapter ProgressReading...
በገና — የዳዊት መሣሪያ

The Begena (በገና) is one of the world's most ancient lyre-type instruments, deeply embedded in Ethiopian Orthodox Christian tradition. Ethiopian oral history and religious texts identify it as the very instrument of King David — the biblical harp with which he composed the Psalms.

"When David played his Begena, the evil spirit left Saul" — Ethiopian interpretation of 1 Samuel 16:23, rendered in the Ge'ez tradition.

Sacred Origins

According to Ethiopian tradition, the Begena was brought to Ethiopia by Menelik I — son of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba (Makeda) — along with the Ark of the Covenant. This origin story gives the instrument an almost unparalleled sacred status in Ethiopian culture.

Unlike most musical instruments used for entertainment or celebration, the Begena is primarily an instrument of meditation and spiritual contemplation. It is played during fasting periods, particularly during Tsome Filseta (the Assumption fast) and Tsome Nebiyat (Prophets' fast).

Historical Context

The instrument reached its height of cultural prominence in the 15th–17th centuries during the Solomonic dynasty. Court musicians (called Begena players or "Aleqa") held honored positions in the imperial court and monastery communities.

🕯️ The Begena Today
The Begena is still played in Ethiopia today, particularly in Addis Ababa and the Amhara highlands. Master players such as Alemu Aga have brought the instrument to international attention, performing at UNESCO events and releasing acclaimed recordings. The instrument is considered a living link to Ethiopia's 3,000-year recorded history.

Cultural Restrictions

Traditionally, the Begena was not to be played for entertainment or in secular settings. Its sound — including the characteristic birr buzzing created by the leather pieces on the bridge — was said to represent the voice of God, and playing it casually was considered disrespectful.

CHAPTER TWO
Anatomy of the Begena
Ten strings, each with spiritual meaning
Pillars (Teret)
ተረት
The two tall carved wooden columns that form the frame. Traditionally made from Wanza wood, they are carved with intricate geometric patterns and represent the two pillars of Solomon's Temple.
Crossbar (Metek)
መጠቅ
The horizontal bar at the top connecting the two pillars. Tuning pegs (Quana) are inserted through this bar to hold and tension each of the 10 strings.
Bridge (Birkuma)
ብርቁማ
The bridge sits on the face of the soundbox and is the source of the famous birr buzz. Small leather pieces (also called birr) rest loosely against the strings here, creating the characteristic buzzing timbre.
Tailpiece
ጅራት
A strip of bone or hardwood at the base of the soundbox where all 10 strings are anchored. The string spacing is widest at the tailpiece.
Strings (Awtar)
አውታር
Traditionally made from sheep or goat gut, the 10 strings fan from the crossbar down to the tailpiece. Each string (Awtar) has a spiritual name corresponding to one of the 10 Commandments.
CHAPTER THREE
Music Theory & St. Yared
The Ethiopian pentatonic tradition and Kignit scales

Saint Yared & Ethiopian Sacred Music

Ethiopian sacred music was codified by Saint Yared (505–571 AD), the legendary composer who is said to have received his musical knowledge from three birds sent from Paradise. He developed a notational system called Zema and established three primary musical modes which influenced all subsequent Ethiopian church music.

Saint Yared created three modes: Ge'ez (for ordinary time), Ezel (for fasting), and Araray (for feast days). The Begena operates primarily within the Ezel mode — music of fasting and reflection.

The Kignit System

Ethiopian music is organized around pentatonic scales called Kignit (ቅኝት). Unlike Western major/minor scales which use 7 notes, Kignit scales use 5 primary pitches with characteristic intervals that give Ethiopian music its distinctive sound.

The Birr Effect — Acoustic Physics

The most distinctive feature of Begena sound is the birr (ብር) buzz — a controlled, intentional rattling sound created by small leather pieces resting on the bridge (Birkuma). When a string vibrates, it causes these leather pieces to vibrate sympathetically, adding a complex overtone-rich buzz on top of the fundamental pitch.

This is acoustically similar to the sitar jawari effect or the African mirliton — but with spiritual significance: the buzz is said to represent the presence of the Holy Spirit in the sound.

CHAPTER FOUR
Playing Technique
Hand position, posture, and plucking method

Holding the Begena

The Begena is held vertically, resting on the player's lap or on a low stand, with the soundbox facing outward toward the listener. The instrument stands roughly the height of a seated adult.

Hand Position

Both hands are used. The right hand typically plucks the upper (higher-pitched) strings while the left hand manages the lower strings, though individual masters vary this technique.

🤚
Right Hand
Index and middle fingers pluck strings with a downward-outward motion. The thumb may damp strings to control sustain. Higher strings (strings 6–10).
✋
Left Hand
Handles the bass strings (strings 1–5) with a similar plucking motion. The fingers approach from behind the strings, pushing forward and releasing.
🧘
Posture
The player sits cross-legged or on a low stool. The Begena leans slightly forward. Shoulders relaxed, arms free to move across the full string range.
🎵
Plucking Style
A single string is plucked and allowed to resonate fully before the next note. Rapid arpeggios across multiple strings are common in transitional passages.

Vocal Accompaniment

The Begena traditionally accompanies meditative poetry and psalm recitation. The player sings or chants in a low, resonant voice — often intoning Ge'ez scripture — while the Begena provides a continuous harmonic and rhythmic foundation.

"The voice and the Begena are inseparable. The instrument speaks; the voice answers." — Traditional teaching

Damping Technique

Because the Begena's strings sustain for several seconds, players use active damping — pressing lightly against a string with the flat of the finger — to control which pitches resonate simultaneously. This creates the characteristic layered, reverberant texture of Begena music.
